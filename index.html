About the CXC index

The CXC index adds up the market value (last trade × shares, the creator's own included) of every listing that's trading and compares it with what the same listings were worth at their IPO prices. It is based at 1,000, so 1,100 means trading listings are worth 10% more together than at their IPOs. Bigger listings move it more. Listings still in their IPO aren't counted, and when a new listing opens it joins at its IPO value. Change shown is since each listing's IPO.

How a listing works

Every step mirrors a company going public, made simple enough for a creator to do in a week.

  1. I

    File

    The creator files 12+ months of revenue and costs from every source and picks how much of the company to sell, say 25%. CX confirms who they are and checks the numbers against uploaded statements.

  2. II

    Take bids

    CX sets a price range from the model value. For a few days anyone can bid for shares inside it. Bids are private.

  3. III

    Price it

    The IPO price is the highest price that sells every share, and everyone pays the same price. If less than half the shares get bids, the IPO is withdrawn and no one pays.

  4. IV

    Trade & get paid

    Shares trade on an order book, like any stock. Each quarter the creator files earnings, and the share of profit their charter promises (50% to 100%) is paid out as a dividend.

Questions

The rest is in the terms, risks and the path to real money.

Is this real money?

Not yet. CX is a preview that runs on play money. Selling shares in a creator's business to the public is a securities offering, so real offerings need an SEC exemption (Regulation Crowdfunding or Regulation A) and a registered funding portal or broker-dealer. CX won't take real money until that's in place.

What does a shareholder actually own?

A slice of the creator's business, treated like a company: its profit, its cash and anything else it owns. Each quarter the share of profit set in the creator's charter (50% to 100%) is paid out to every share, the creator's own included, and the rest stays in the business. If the creator closes the company, its cash is paid out to every share. Shares never expire. Shareholders get no votes and no say over content.

What does a creator give up?

The part of their company they sell, 10% to 50%. They keep the rest, their channel, their content rights and full creative control. What they pay themselves counts as a cost, so it comes before profit.

What are the fees?

Two. A 5% listing fee on the money raised in an IPO, and 0.5% of each trade, paid by the order that trades against one already waiting in the book (in the opening auction both sides pay). There are no account, deposit, withdrawal or membership fees, and CX takes nothing from dividends. For comparison, crowdfunding portals usually charge creators 7–10% of a raise, and private-share marketplaces charge up to 5% a side on trades. In the preview all of it is play money.

How is a creator valued?

Like a company: a year of profit times a price-to-earnings ratio, plus the cash in the business. The ratio comes from a risk rate (15% a year, plus up to 10% for sponsor-heavy revenue and up to 10% for revenue that hasn't been verified) and stays between 3× and 18×. Sponsor money is less certain than platform payouts, so it counts for a little less.

How is this different from GigaStar?

GigaStar lets fans invest in a share of a YouTube channel's ad revenue. CX treats the whole creator as a company: every platform plus sponsors and merch, and shares that never expire. For the median YouTube creator in CX Research, about 70% of estimated revenue comes from outside YouTube ads. CX also gives every creator a public valuation before they list.

Six more questions
What if a creator stops posting or their revenue drops?

Profit and dividends drop with it. A loss pays nothing, and it has to be made up before dividends start again. That's the main risk, and it's why prices move. Creators have to file a quarterly report; late filers are flagged, and the exchange can halt trading. IPO money goes into the business, not the creator's pocket.

Who can join?

Anyone can browse CX Markets and CX Research without an account. To trade you open an account, like at any broker: confirm your email, give your legal name and date of birth (you need to be 18 or older), answer a few questions about your finances and pass a short risk check. A creator can only list themselves, or list with the creator's written permission, and proves it with a code in their bio before anything goes live.

Where do the numbers come from?

Two places. CX Research estimates revenue from public stats (the YouTube Data API, TikTok public profiles and Twitch public pages) with the assumptions shown on its Method page, so its figures are ranges, not reported earnings. Listings on CX Markets use what the creator files, and each revenue source shows whether the exchange could check it against a payout statement. The example listings are simulated.

What happens if a creator stops filing?

A CX-Q report is due 91 days after the last one. After that the listing is marked overdue, and 30 days later it's flagged as a late filer. The exchange can halt trading. No report means no dividend for that quarter.

How are dividends paid in the preview?

When a creator files a CX-Q, the share of the quarter's profit set in their charter (50% to 100%, chosen when they file to list) is split across every share, the creator's included, and lands in each holder's play-money cash straight away. Earlier losses are made up first, and the payout is cut if it would leave the business with less than one quarter's costs in cash. That percentage is a share of profit, not a return on what you paid.

What data does CX keep?

Your email, username and a scrambled (hashed) password, what you give when opening your account (legal name, date of birth, state and your answers about your finances), one sign-in cookie, your settings, and your trades, bids and dividends. No ads, no tracking or analytics scripts, and nothing is sold. You can download or delete your data in Settings on CX Markets. Read the privacy section.
